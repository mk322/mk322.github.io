tldr: |
  - **Problem:** Multimodal reasoning often inherits the representations of its teachers: a visual step stays visual, a state step stays a state vector. What is easy to describe in each format may differ from what the next decision needs.
  - **Our solution:** **Uni-LaDiR** learns one shared latent space for intermediate thoughts. **Continuation prediction** trains thoughts to support later reasoning; jointly trained **diffusion** learns to generate them from the available context.
  - **Key insight:** The format that teaches a model does not have to be the format in which it thinks. A useful thought must carry information forward and be something the model can produce on its own.

Suppose a robot is about to pick up a mug. Its camera sees the handle. A point cloud describes the space around it. Its joint state tells it where the gripper is. Before moving, it needs to answer one fairly ordinary question: **will my fingers fit?**

That question belongs to none of those modalities in particular. It is a relation between the mug, the surrounding space, and the hand. We can describe it in words, sketch it, or express it in coordinates. The description changes; the decision we need to make does not.

This is the starting point of Uni-LaDiR: **how information is observed need not decide how it participates in thinking. What comes next should help decide what a thought contains.**

{% include blog/uni-ladir/decision.html %}

It sounds natural until we try to teach a model to do it. Training data gives us things we can inspect: sentences, images, geometry, states. A straightforward approach is to teach an intermediate thought to reproduce one of those things. Now we have a concrete target and a measurable loss. But we have also slipped in an assumption: being good at reproducing a description makes that representation good for carrying a decision forward.

Those goals can pull in different directions. Reconstructing the mug's image rewards retaining its appearance. Preparing the next grasp may instead require retaining the space between the handle and the fingers. Both use the same observation. They ask different things of it.

We built **Uni-LaDiR** around that distinction. Teacher steps from different modalities enter one learned thought space. Rather than defining a thought by what it reconstructs, we train it through what later reasoning can do with it. A latent diffusion model then learns to generate these thoughts without the teacher. To our knowledge, this is the first framework to combine a shared thought space learned through continuation prediction with latent diffusion generation across visual reasoning and robot control. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

The shared space is only half the problem. A useful thought that the model cannot produce by itself is a good teaching signal, but a poor reasoning procedure. The rest of this post follows how those two requirements—**use it** and **generate it**—lead to the method, and what the experiments actually tell us.

[Paper →](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19878v3)

## Reading several modalities is not yet thinking across them

A multimodal model can read several kinds of input without sharing the representations of its intermediate thoughts. LaST₀, for example, combines visual, 3D, and state reasoning, but each modality retains its own latent format. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-8" aria-label="Reference 8">[8]</a></sup> Concatenating those blocks gives the model access to them in one sequence. Predicting the next block still means predicting in the space associated with that step's modality.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/world.html %}

Uni-LaDiR changes that intermediate interface. All teacher steps are mapped into the same learned space, and successive thoughts are generated there. The thought's content changes as reasoning advances; its format remains shared. Specialized encoders still read images, point clouds, and states. Their features become inputs to the shared encoder during training. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

Sharing a format does not require every modality to convey the same information. A caption may identify the mug while geometry reveals clearance the caption omits. Nor do we require two observations of the same fact to produce identical vectors. The goal is for their useful information to support later reasoning through one common interface.

This is related to the broader question of representational alignment raised by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-2" aria-label="Reference 2">[2]</a></sup> Here, we learn the space for a specific purpose: predicting how reasoning continues. The closest VLM baseline, Mull-Tokens, already uses modality-agnostic latent tokens, but trains each step toward text tokens or frozen image features. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-16" aria-label="Reference 16">[16]</a></sup> Uni-LaDiR learns the latent targets themselves from their downstream use.

## What should survive from one step to the next?

Once we separate the teacher’s format from the thought’s purpose, the design question becomes concrete: how do we learn a compact representation that the next step can actually use? Figure 2 shows the complete training setup. We will unpack it from the information that has to cross each boundary. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/grounding.html %}

### First, give the teachers a common interface

Let a **teacher step** be one piece of a training reasoning trace: a sentence, an intermediate image, a point cloud, or a robot state. A modality encoder first turns it into features, denoted <em>s<sub>i</sub></em>. We append learnable queries <em>q</em>, pass the step through the shared LLM encoder, and project the query outputs into a block of thought tokens <em>z<sub>i</sub><sup>★</sup></em>.

Every modality uses the same queries and output projection. In our experiments, a block contains **four 512-dimensional tokens**. An attention mask allows each query block to read only its own teacher step and itself. It cannot read the task input or other teacher steps while constructing that block. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 3.2; Appendix B.2.)

Each block therefore summarizes one local teaching step. The input and earlier blocks provide the wider context when the reasoner uses it. This distinction matters: we are not asking each block to reconstruct a complete world state.

### A common shape still needs a reason to keep information

A shared shape is easy to specify. Useful contents have to be learned.

We train through **continuation prediction**. Given the task input and the earlier thought blocks, the backbone predicts the next teacher step. Given the entire latent chain, it predicts the final answer or action. Raw earlier teacher steps are hidden from these predictions, as are future latent blocks. Information from a previous teacher step must reach the next prediction through its thought tokens. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 3.3; Appendix A.1.)

For the mug, an earlier block might need to retain a spatial relation that helps predict the next stage of a grasp. Copying the source appearance would reward retaining what makes the image reproducible. Predicting the continuation rewards retaining what makes the later step predictable. The task supplies a reason to keep one detail over another.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/handoff.html %}

The targets remain concrete: text uses cross-entropy; continuous image, 3D, and state targets use squared error. The final output is a text answer for visual reasoning, or an action sequence produced by a flow-matching head for control. The **latent targets are learned**; the teacher targets used for supervision remain fixed.

### The missing piece: the teacher will not be there at test time

A teacher can reveal information the model will not have at inference. For example, a future image from a successful demonstration can show that a grasp worked. Encoding it may yield a useful training target, but predicting that target from the current observation is a separate problem.

We use diffusion to learn a conditional distribution over the next thought block:

<div class="uni-equation" role="math" aria-label="Sample the next thought block given the task input and earlier blocks">z<sub>i</sub> ∼ p<sub>θ</sub>(z<sub>i</sub> | x, z<sub>&lt;i</sub>)</div>

Here, <em>x</em> is the task input and <em>z<sub>&lt;i</sub></em> contains the preceding thoughts. The same context can admit several useful continuations. In the mug example, different feasible grasp approaches could require different next steps. Predicting an average with squared-error regression can miss those alternatives. This motivates a distribution over thoughts; diffusion provides a way to generate it while updating all tokens in a block together.

During training, we mix a clean target block with Gaussian noise and train the model to predict the velocity along the noise-to-thought path. This is **flow matching**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-3" aria-label="Reference 3">[3]</a></sup> During inference, we start from noise and integrate the learned updates to produce a block. The input and preceding blocks guide every update. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 3.4.)

There are two distinct kinds of steps here. **Denoising steps** construct the current block. **Reasoning steps** add completed blocks to the chain. Once a block is completed, it stays fixed while the next is generated. Diffusion here does not revise the entire past chain.

### A moving target can be an advantage

If we learn the encoder first and freeze it, the reasoner must fit whatever targets that encoder chose. Those targets may be good for continuation prediction yet difficult to generate from the available input.

Joint training lets the two adapt through their shared backbone weights. Continuation loss trains the representation to be useful; diffusion loss trains the backbone to predict it. Updating the shared weights also changes how teacher steps are encoded. This couples **what the model needs to retain** with **what it can learn to generate**.

The clean diffusion targets and conditioning prefix are detached from diffusion gradients. Joint training still updates the shared backbone through both losses. It does not pass the answer loss through sampled denoising trajectories. The two objectives shape the shared system through their respective training paths. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

At inference, the teacher encoding path is removed. The model generates thought blocks from the input, decides when to stop the chain, and predicts the answer or action. It never needs to render an intermediate image or retrieve a future teacher state.

## Does the common interface make a difference?

The design sounds plausible. But the results need to distinguish three things that are easy to conflate: having more teachers, giving their information a common interface, and learning that interface for the right purpose. We start with the full system, then remove those explanations one at a time.

Visual reasoning uses Qwen2.5-VL-7B <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-4" aria-label="Reference 4">[4]</a></sup> with text and image teacher traces from Zebra-CoT. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-5" aria-label="Reference 5">[5]</a></sup> Robot control uses LIBERO <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-6" aria-label="Reference 6">[6]</a></sup> and RLBench <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-7" aria-label="Reference 7">[7]</a></sup>, with image and state teachers plus 3D point clouds on RLBench. These are separate model instances using the same design. All results and original paper figures below come from **arXiv v3, September 29, 2026**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

### Start with the practical question: does it help?

We first compare Uni-LaDiR with the base model and prior reasoners, measuring visual accuracy and robot task success.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/main-results.html %}

On seven vision-centric benchmarks, mean accuracy rises from **64.6% to 78.5%** over the Qwen2.5-VL base model. The strongest latent baseline in this group, Mull-Tokens, reaches **74.9%**. On four visual math and logic benchmarks, Uni-LaDiR reaches **49.0%**, compared with ILVR's **45.7%**, a **7.3% relative gain**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">[9]</a></sup> These compare complete VLM methods: they share the backbone and evaluation examples, but use different training data and recipes. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Table 2; Appendix B.1.)

On RLBench, success increases from LaST₀'s **82% to 87%**, improving nine of ten tasks and matching the remaining one. Control frequency is **16.5 Hz**, versus **15.4 Hz** for LaST₀ and **1.1 Hz** for CoT-VLA. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-12" aria-label="Reference 12">[12]</a></sup> The timing uses the same RTX 4090, without action chunking, and excludes simulator stepping and rendering. On LIBERO, mean success is **99.2%** across four suites. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Tables 1 and 3; Appendix B.2.)

The improvement is not universal: MathVista <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-10" aria-label="Reference 10">[10]</a></sup> is **73.2%**, slightly below Vision-R1's **73.5%**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-11" aria-label="Reference 11">[11]</a></sup>

The complete method helps in both settings, and the RLBench gain comes without a loss of control frequency. That still leaves the most obvious alternative explanation: perhaps the model just benefits from receiving several kinds of teaching signal. To test the interface itself, we have to keep those teachers fixed.

### Keep the teachers. Change what they share.

All variants in this study receive the same teacher modalities. We keep the backbone, data, and continuation supervision fixed, and change which modalities use the shared encoder. Thought-token count and width remain the same. Separate encoders have at least as many encoder parameters as the unified model, so this comparison does not match parameter counts. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 4.3; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/results.html %}

Unifying text and image increases mean accuracy on V* <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">[13]</a></sup>, MMVP <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-14" aria-label="Reference 14">[14]</a></sup>, MathVista, and EMMA <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-15" aria-label="Reference 15">[15]</a></sup> from **63.0% to 70.1%**: **11.3% higher in relative terms**. On RLBench, sharing any two of the three modalities reaches **83.5–84.5%** success. Sharing all three reaches **87.0%**, versus **82.0%** with separate encoders. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 3.)

The same teachers become more useful when they share an interface; adding supervision cannot explain this comparison. Full sharing also outperforms every pair. We cannot yet separate semantic alignment from the optimization benefits of sharing an encoder. But we can now ask a sharper question: **once we have a common space, what should train its contents?**

## Sharing is useful. What should the shared space learn?

We cross two choices: separate versus unified encoding, and reconstruction versus continuation prediction. Learned variants use the same data, training steps, diffusion loss, and final-output supervision. A frozen-target control tests whether learning the latent targets helps at all. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 4.4; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/objective.html %}

With unified encoding, continuation prediction improves the six-benchmark mean by **9.2 percentage points** over reconstruction. On RLBench, it raises success from **77.3% to 87.0%**. With separate encoders, it also improves the mean, by **6.6 points**. The six-benchmark summary combines four visual benchmarks with LIBERO and RLBench. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 5.)

The plot also shows where the average can mislead: under continuation prediction, unification lowers MathVista from **75.5% to 73.2%** and EMMA from **36.2% to 35.6%**, while improving the other four tasks.

This is the experiment that bears most directly on our opening question. With either encoding design, training a thought for its continuation works better on average than training it to reproduce its source. A shared space helps overall, but the objective tells it what to be useful *for*. Neither choice guarantees a gain on every task.

There is a catch. So far, we have learned the targets using teacher information. A thought can help continuation precisely because the teacher supplied something the model would struggle to infer. We still need the model to reach useful targets on its own.

## A useful target is not automatically a learnable target

We compare separate-stage training, which freezes the learned encoder before training the diffusion reasoner, with joint training. The unified architecture and continuation supervision stay the same. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/joint-training.html %}

Joint training improves the six-benchmark mean by **12.7% relative**, including **15.0% relative** on RLBench and **8.8%** on EMMA. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 4.)

Freezing the representation first makes a tidy training pipeline, but gives worse task performance here. The result supports letting the representation and its generator adapt together. It does not directly measure target predictability, so we should not claim that every target becomes easier to generate. The practical finding is narrower: the full system works better when we stop treating target construction as a finished job.

### Could we simply predict the next block in one pass?

A simpler generator could predict the next clean block in one pass. We compare direct prediction with L2 or cosine loss against flow matching. All three use the unified encoder, joint training, the same continuation supervision, and the same conditioning context. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/diffusion.html %}

Flow matching improves the six-benchmark mean by **16.3% relative to L2 prediction** and **21.0% relative to cosine prediction**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 6.)

The simpler predictors are reasonable alternatives, but diffusion gives better downstream results in these comparisons. That earns its place in this system. It does not prove that modeling multiple valid continuations is the cause of the gain, or that diffusion wins at every matched latency budget. The mug example motivates a distribution over thoughts; this experiment tests the generation procedures.

## One last check: could the model be ignoring its thoughts?

A latent chain could be present while the model answers mostly from the input. We test that possibility by modifying the generated chain while keeping the input and sequence length unchanged. We recompute the output with fresh hidden states. For robot control, the intervention is applied at every reasoning update under the same initial scene. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Appendix B.4.)

| Generated chain | V* accuracy (%) | RLBench success (%) |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Intact | **89.5** | **87.0** |
| Zeroed | 55.8 | 55.0 |
| Blocks shuffled | 65.8 | 65.0 |
| Replaced with another example's chain | 50.8 | 51.0 |

Zeroing removes the contents; shuffling preserves the blocks but changes their order; replacement supplies thoughts from the wrong example. Each hurts performance substantially. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Table 7.)

The model depends on which thoughts it receives and in what order. Keeping the shape and length while inserting the wrong example’s thoughts is especially damaging. This closes an important loophole: the latent chain is not merely extra computation that the final prediction can ignore. It carries example-specific information the model uses. We still cannot read an individual token and identify the physical relation it represents.

## What the format leaves open

A camera, a sentence, and a state vector do not offer the same evidence. We still need specialized ways to read them. What they need not dictate is the format of every intermediate step after we have read them.

The experiments support a concrete way to make that separation: share the thought interface, learn its contents through continuation, and train a generator alongside it. Changing the learned chain then changes the answer or action. Together, these results go beyond giving several modalities the same token shape. They show that we can learn an intermediate interface around the job of carrying reasoning forward. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

The next question is reuse. If a geometric relation was learned from a point cloud, can a thought learned from an image substitute for it in a new setting? We have not established that kind of interchangeability. Teacher quality and the size of each teaching step may also change what the space learns. Those tests would tell us whether this interface carries useful abstractions beyond the traces that taught it.

For the robot reaching toward the mug, the immediate requirement is simpler: carry the right information into the next decision. Uni-LaDiR gives us evidence that the teaching format can remain a source of supervision without becoming the boundary of thought.
