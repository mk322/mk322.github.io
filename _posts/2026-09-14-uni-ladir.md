A robot sees a mug beside a book. Its camera captures appearance; a point cloud describes geometry; its internal state records the gripper configuration. A sentence might simply say, “Pick up the mug.”

These observations become useful together. Recognizing the mug does not establish that the fingers can reach its handle. Knowing that the gripper is closed does not establish that it holds the mug. **The decision lives in the relationships between observations.**

Our motivation for Uni-LaDiR came from both practical failures and a modeling intuition: these modalities describe a common, evolving situation. We wanted the model's intermediate reasoning to carry that situation forward without having to express each new thought in a particular medium. The mug below is an illustrative example of this concern.

## Where information becomes fragmented

A natural approach is to give each modality its own representation, then let a shared model reason over their sequence. This preserves useful specialization: image features need not look like language tokens, and robot coordinates retain their structure. Attention can already connect these representations. Separate encoders are not inherently a problem.

The harder question is what happens **between reasoning steps**. Suppose an intermediate image suggests that a gripper is near a mug, and the next step predicts a robot state. That transition has to determine what should happen next while also translating visual evidence into a different representational vocabulary. The relation that matters—whether the fingers are positioned to secure this object—must survive that translation.

This is the fragmentation we care about. A system can retain the object, the fingers, and their individual features while failing to maintain the relation needed for the next decision. Plausible outputs in each modality are an insufficient objective: a plausible image and a plausible gripper state can describe an implausible combination.

There is a second, subtler issue with the sequence. An image, a geometric description, and a robot state may refer to the same instant. Ordering them as image → 3D → state is a choice about computation; it does not make one observation the physical cause of the next. Such a factorization is mathematically valid. What we wanted to relax was the requirement that advancing a thought also means choosing and producing its next modality-specific expression.

The [Platonic Representation Hypothesis](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/huh24a.html) offers a useful connection. Huh and colleagues study representational alignment across models and modalities, and hypothesize convergence toward a shared statistical description of reality. They also emphasize that different sensors carry different information. We take this as motivation for a design question: **can different observations train an internal representation that later computation can use in a common way?** It is not a guarantee that all modalities should become identical.

In fact, forcing identical information would defeat the purpose. Geometry may reveal clearance that a caption omits; robot state may reveal something invisible to the camera. A useful shared workspace must accommodate these differences while making their consequences accessible to the same reasoner.

## What should a thought preserve?

“One world” is a starting intuition, but reconstructing the entire world is an unnecessarily demanding training objective. For picking up the mug, its relation to the gripper matters more than the texture of the book. For reading the book, the priorities change. The representation we want is **shaped by what the task will need next**.

Uni-LaDiR puts this into practice with a common latent interface. During training, modality-specific encoders turn teacher steps into features. A shared backbone compresses each step into the same-sized block of latent tokens, using learnable queries that can read only that step. The blocks share an output projection and a downstream consumer.

A block is therefore a local piece of teaching information, not a complete world state. The task input and accumulated latent prefix provide the context for subsequent reasoning. “Workspace” refers to this common computational interface; it does not imply that we have recovered an explicit simulator of reality.

Giving vectors the same shape is only the beginning. We train the latent prefix to help predict later teacher steps and the final answer or action, while hiding the earlier raw teacher steps from those predictions. This asks the constructor to preserve information that another computation will need. Reconstructing the source would instead reward preserving whatever makes that source easy to reproduce.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/grounding.html %}

The distinction changes how I think about intermediate supervision. An image or a state trace can teach the model something useful without becoming something the model must render on its way to an answer. Teachers provide evidence about how a task can be solved; they also help shape the interface in which the model learns to reason.

That leaves another requirement. A latent supplied by a teacher might be wonderfully informative and impossible to predict from the available input. At inference, the model has to produce its own states. Usefulness and generatability have to be learned together.

## One world still leaves many possible next thoughts

The world may have one underlying state, but our observations do not uniquely identify it. A handle can be occluded. Contact can be uncertain. Even with complete information, more than one approach to a task may work. A common representation should leave room for that uncertainty and choice.

This is where diffusion enters Uni-LaDiR. We model a distribution over the next latent block, conditioned on the task input and the preceding blocks:

<div class="uni-equation" role="math" aria-label="The next latent block is sampled from a conditional distribution given the input and previous latent blocks">z<sub>i</sub> ∼ p<sub>θ</sub>(z<sub>i</sub> | x, z<sub>&lt;i</sub>)</div>

Here, <em>x</em> is the task input, and <em>z<sub>&lt;i</sub></em> is the reasoning already generated. Diffusion constructs a sample through repeated denoising updates within the new block. The same generation rule applies regardless of which teacher modality helped construct the training target.

A simple geometric analogy explains the appeal of a distribution. If two valid approaches go around opposite sides of an obstacle, averaging them can produce a path through it. The analogy does not establish the geometry of learned latents, but it makes the modeling requirement concrete: multiple valid continuations should not have to collapse into one compromise. Diffusion is our choice for expressing that conditional distribution; other expressive generative models can also represent multiple possibilities.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/diffusion.html %}

There are two distinct kinds of progress in this animation. Denoising refines the current block; completing a block advances the reasoning sequence. Uni-LaDiR still generates blocks in order, conditioning each one on the previously generated prefix. It does not denoise the whole history again, and these computational steps are not a clock for the physical world.

During training, grounding and diffusion share a backbone. Grounding shapes what the latent constructor retains; flow matching learns how to generate the resulting targets, which are recomputed as the constructor changes. The targets are detached on the diffusion branch. Joint training here does **not** mean backpropagating the answer loss through sampled denoising trajectories. At inference, teacher traces are neither supplied nor decoded: the generated latent sequence conditions the answer or action.

This combination is the project’s central bet. The workspace makes heterogeneous supervision usable through a common interface. Diffusion learns possible continuations in that interface. Their value depends on whether those continuations preserve what the downstream task needs.

## The comparison that matters to this story

A stronger model with more teachers would not, by itself, tell us whether sharing matters. For that question, we keep the teachers, backbone, total latent budget, trainable parameters, training schedule, and inference compute fixed, and change how the modalities share the latent interface.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/results.html %}

With text and image teachers, mean accuracy on four visual-reasoning benchmarks rises from **62.97% to 67.66%** when the interface is shared. With visual, 3D, and robot-state teachers, mean success across LIBERO and RLBench rises from **90.05% to 93.08%**. Sharing any two of those three modalities produces a smaller improvement than sharing all three. These are separate visual-reasoning and robot-control settings, rather than evidence that one checkpoint handles every task.

These results support learning a common interface under the tested conditions. They do not yet establish that the model internally represents the particular object–contact relations in our opening example. Shared parameters may also help through regularization or easier optimization. The representation-level explanation remains something to test more directly.

Two further comparisons make the design more specific. Across the draft’s six-task ablation average, training latents for downstream use improves performance by **5.9% relative** to source reconstruction; joint training improves it by **2.9% relative** to separately training construction and generation. The lesson is that sharing needs an objective and a generator that make the shared states useful—not just a common vector width.

The next test I care about is functional: if two observations convey the same task-relevant information, can a latent constructed from one replace a latent constructed from the other without disrupting the next decision? And when one observation contains extra information, does the workspace preserve its effect? This would probe both reuse and the differences that sharing must not erase.

We began with several views of a mug. The useful internal state would let the model carry their joint implications into its next decision, including what remains uncertain. That is the direction behind Uni-LaDiR: choose an interface around the reasoning that must continue, and learn a distribution over how it can continue.
