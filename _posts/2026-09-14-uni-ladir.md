<div id="motivation"></div>

Human thinking connects what we see, hear, and read. A photo of a dog, its bark, and a written description can all connect to our knowledge of dogs. The **hub-and-spoke** account in cognitive science offers an explanation: shared concepts connect information from different senses. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-16">[16]</a></sup><sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-17">[17]</a></sup> **Could an AI model also learn from different modalities and reason through a common space?**

The challenge appears when a model needs to carry information from one step to the next. A robot planning a grasp may use an image to find a mug's handle, 3D geometry to judge clearance, and its own state to plan the hand's movement. These are parts of one decision. Yet existing reasoning chains can represent each step in a separate modality-specific space, as in LaST₀. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup> Moving the reasoning forward then also requires predicting across these different formats. A shared thought space would give every step the same interface for passing useful information to the next.

**Uni-LaDiR learns that shared thought space, and using it improves reasoning.** With the same teaching data, sharing the encoder across text and images improves mean accuracy by **11.3% relative** on four visual benchmarks. The same design also improves robot control when images, 3D geometry, and robot states share an encoder. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> The key is how we learn the space: each thought is trained to retain what later reasoning needs.

## Learn the shared space through reasoning {#unification}

A shared token format gives us somewhere to put the thoughts. We still need a rule for deciding what goes into them.

One option is to train each thought to reproduce the step that supplied it: reconstruct image features from an image step, or predict text from a text step. This gives a clear target, but keeps that target tied to the source modality. Mull-Tokens, for example, uses modality-agnostic latent tokens supervised by text tokens or frozen image features. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-15">[15]</a></sup>

We learn the contents through **continuation prediction**. A training trace supplies intermediate steps, which we call *teacher steps*. We encode each one into a compact block of latent thought tokens, then train those blocks to help predict later teacher steps and the final answer or action. For the mug, a thought should retain the handle's location and the clearance needed for the next part of the grasp. The continuation provides a concrete learning signal for keeping that information. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/decision.html %}

**The source tells us where the evidence came from; the continuation tells us what the thought needs to carry.** This gives all modalities a common learning objective as well as a common format. Different steps can preserve different facts while contributing to the same reasoning process.

At test time, the model must generate these thoughts itself. Uni-LaDiR trains a **diffusion reasoner** alongside the shared encoder, so the space learns to support reasoning while the reasoner learns to generate its contents. To our knowledge, Uni-LaDiR is the first framework to combine a continuation-trained shared thought space with latent diffusion across visual reasoning and robot control. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

[Paper →](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19878)

## The method: thoughts we can use and generate {#method}

The method turns that idea into three components, shown in Figure 2: **Latent Encoding**, **Continuation Prediction**, and **Diffusion Training**. Together, they define the thoughts, train their contents, and learn to generate them. **Joint Training** lets all three learn through the same backbone. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/grounding.html %}

### 1. Latent Encoding {#latent-encoding}

A **teacher step** can be a sentence, an intermediate image, a point cloud, or a robot state. A modality encoder first turns it into features, denoted <em>s<sub>i</sub></em>. We append learnable queries <em>q</em>: tokens trained to collect information from the step. The features and queries pass through the shared language-model encoder. We then project the query outputs into a block of thought tokens <em>z<sub>i</sub><sup>★</sup></em>.

Every modality uses the same queries and output projection. In our experiments, a block contains **four 512-dimensional tokens**. An attention mask allows each query block to read only its own teacher step and itself. It cannot read the task input or other teacher steps while constructing that block. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 3.2; Appendix B.2.)

Each block therefore encodes one teacher step. The task input and earlier blocks provide the wider context when the reasoner uses it.

### 2. Continuation Prediction {#continuation-prediction}

We train through **continuation prediction**. Given the task input and the earlier thought blocks, the backbone predicts the next teacher step. Given the entire latent chain, it predicts the final answer or action. Raw earlier teacher steps are hidden from these predictions, as are future latent blocks. Information from a previous teacher step must reach the next prediction through its thought tokens. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 3.3; Appendix A.1.)

This creates an information bottleneck: whatever a later prediction needs from an earlier teacher step must survive in that step’s thought block. For a grasp, that could be the handle’s location relative to the hand. The loss rewards keeping the details that help the next prediction.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/handoff.html %}

The targets remain concrete: text uses cross-entropy; continuous image, 3D, and state targets use squared error. The final output is a text answer for visual reasoning, or an action sequence produced by a flow-matching head for control. The **latent targets are learned**; the teacher targets used for supervision remain fixed.

### 3. Diffusion Training {#diffusion-training}

A teacher can reveal information the model will not have at inference. For example, a future image from a successful demonstration can show that a grasp worked. Encoding it may yield a useful training target, but predicting that target from the current observation is a separate problem.

We use diffusion to learn a conditional distribution over the next thought block:

<div class="uni-equation" role="math" aria-label="Sample the next thought block given the task input and earlier blocks">z<sub>i</sub> ∼ p<sub>θ</sub>(z<sub>i</sub> | x, z<sub>&lt;i</sub>)</div>

Here, <em>x</em> is the task input and <em>z<sub>&lt;i</sub></em> contains the preceding thoughts. The same context can admit several useful continuations. In the mug example, different feasible grasp approaches could require different next steps. Predicting an average with squared-error regression can miss those alternatives. Diffusion can learn this distribution and generate a block by updating all of its tokens together.

During training, we mix a clean target block with Gaussian noise and train the model to predict the velocity along the noise-to-thought path. This is **flow matching**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-3" aria-label="Reference 3">[3]</a></sup> During inference, we start from noise and integrate the learned updates to produce a block. The input and preceding blocks guide every update. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 3.4.)

At inference, the model generates thought blocks from the input, decides when to stop the chain, and predicts the answer or action. The teacher steps are needed only during training.

### Joint Training {#joint-training}

**We train the encoder and diffusion reasoner jointly through a shared backbone.** This matters because learning the encoder first and freezing it would leave the reasoner fitting whatever targets that encoder chose. Those targets may support continuation prediction yet be difficult to generate from the available input.

The joint objective combines **continuation and final-output supervision** with the **diffusion loss**. The former trains the thoughts to support later reasoning and the task output; the latter trains the backbone to generate them. Updating the shared weights also changes how teacher steps are encoded. This couples **what the model needs to retain** with **what it can learn to generate**.

During a diffusion update, the clean target block and earlier thought blocks are treated as fixed targets and context, using stop-gradient. The shared backbone still learns from both objectives: continuation and final-output losses train the encoded thoughts, while the diffusion loss trains their generator. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>


## Testing the design: six research questions {#experiments}

We first test the complete system, then examine sharing, continuation supervision, joint training, diffusion, and the generated thoughts themselves. Each experiment below gives its own setup, results, and conclusion. Visual reasoning and robot control use separate model instances of the same design. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

### RQ1. Does unified latent reasoning improve visual answers and robot actions? {#rq1}

**Setup.** We test the complete system in two settings:

- **Visual reasoning.** Train Qwen2.5-VL-7B <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> on Zebra-CoT <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup> traces containing text and intermediate images. Compare with the base model and prior reasoning methods using the same backbone and evaluation questions. Measure final-answer accuracy on **seven vision-centric benchmarks** and **four visual math/logic benchmarks**.
- **Robot control.** Use a 3.3B mixture-of-transformers policy with two Janus-Pro-1.5B experts and an action head. Train on **100 demonstrations per task for ten RLBench tasks** <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>, and **500 demonstrations per suite for LIBERO's four ten-task suites**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup> We train a separate policy for each LIBERO suite. Future images and robot states provide teacher steps during training; RLBench also supplies 3D point clouds. The RLBench comparison follows LaST₀'s backbone, demonstration, training, and evaluation protocol.

At test time, both models generate their own thoughts from the task input, without teacher steps. Robot performance is task success: **20 rollouts per RLBench task** and **50 per LIBERO task**, for each of three seeds. Control frequency is measured on one RTX 4090 under the same timing protocol, without action chunking. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Tables 1–3; Appendix B.1.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/main-results.html %}

**Results.** Seven-benchmark vision accuracy rises from **64.6% to 78.5%** over the base model; Mull-Tokens reaches **74.9%**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-15">[15]</a></sup> On four visual math/logic benchmarks, Uni-LaDiR reaches **49.0%**, versus ILVR's **45.7%**: a **7.3% relative gain**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup> <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, Table 2]</a></sup>

RLBench success rises from LaST₀'s **82% to 87%**, with nine of ten tasks improved and the remaining task matched. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup> Uni-LaDiR runs at **16.5 Hz**, versus **15.4 Hz** for LaST₀ and **1.1 Hz** for CoT-VLA. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-11">[11]</a></sup> Timing uses the same RTX 4090 without action chunking and excludes simulator stepping and rendering. LIBERO mean success is **99.2%** across four suites. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Conclusion.** Unified latent reasoning raises average visual reasoning accuracy and RLBench task success, while running at **16.5 Hz** on RLBench.

### RQ2. Does sharing a thought space help when the teachers stay the same? {#rq2}

**Setup.** Train Qwen2.5-VL-7B on Zebra-CoT for visual reasoning, and a 3.3B Janus-Pro robot policy on RLBench demonstrations. Keep every teacher modality available and change **which modalities share an encoder**:

- **Visual reasoning:** compare separate text and image encoders with one shared encoder.
- **RLBench:** compare three separate encoders with pairwise sharing—image + 3D, image + state, or 3D + state—and finally one encoder shared by all three. In each pairwise variant, the remaining modality keeps its own encoder.

Every variant uses the same backbone, training data, teacher supervision, continuation objective for predicting later steps, and thought budget: **four 512-dimensional tokens per block**. Each separate encoder has the same architecture as the shared encoder. We measure mean accuracy on **V*, MMVP, MathVista, and EMMA**, and task success on **RLBench**, averaged over three seeds. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 4.3; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/results.html %}

**Results.** Sharing text and image increases mean accuracy on V* <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup>, MMVP <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-13">[13]</a></sup>, MathVista <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup>, and EMMA <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-14">[14]</a></sup> from **63.0% to 70.1%**, an **11.3% relative gain**. On RLBench, sharing two modalities reaches **83.5–84.5%** success. Sharing all three reaches **87.0%**, compared with **82.0%** for separate encoders. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 3.)

**Conclusion.** **Sharing the encoder improves reasoning with the same teacher data.** On RLBench, sharing all three modalities gives the highest success rate.

Sharing helps. Does the objective used to learn that shared space also matter?

### RQ3. Unified or separate—and continuation or reconstruction? {#rq3}

**Setup.** Test Qwen2.5-VL-7B trained on Zebra-CoT and 3.3B Janus-Pro robot policies trained on their respective LIBERO or RLBench demonstrations. Cross **two encoder choices** with **two learning objectives**:

| Variant | How modalities are encoded | What each thought is trained to predict |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Separate + Reconstruction | One encoder per modality | Its source teacher features |
| Unified + Reconstruction | One encoder shared across modalities | Its source teacher features |
| Separate + Continuation | One encoder per modality | Later teacher steps |
| Unified + Continuation | One encoder shared across modalities | Later teacher steps |

All four learn the encoder jointly with the reasoner, using the same data, training steps, four 512-dimensional tokens per block, diffusion loss, and final-answer/action supervision. Separate encoders use the same architecture, query structure, and output shape as the shared encoder. A fifth variant, **Frozen (separate)**, fixes the modality encoders and trains only the reasoner.

Evaluate **V*, MMVP, MathVista, EMMA, LIBERO, and RLBench**. The first four measure answer accuracy; the last two measure task success. The six-benchmark mean gives each benchmark equal weight and averages results over three seeds. This lets us compare sharing at a fixed objective, then compare objectives at a fixed encoder design. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 4.4; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/objective.html %}

**Results.** Read the figure in two directions:

- **Unified vs. separate, holding the objective fixed.** Across the six benchmarks, unification improves the mean by **3.5 percentage points** under reconstruction and **6.1 points** under continuation. On RLBench, it raises success from **73.0% to 77.3%** under reconstruction, and from **82.0% to 87.0%** under continuation prediction. The common encoder helps in this setting under either objective. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>
- **Continuation vs. reconstruction, holding the encoder fixed.** Continuation prediction improves the six-benchmark mean by **6.6 percentage points** with separate encoding and **9.2 points** with unified encoding. Learning a thought through its downstream use helps under either encoder design. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 5.)

**Conclusion.** **Unified encoding and continuation prediction each improve the six-benchmark mean. Combining them gives the best average.**

The encoder learns which thoughts would help; the generator must learn to produce them. Should they adapt together?

### RQ4. Should the thought encoder and diffusion reasoner learn together? {#rq4}

**Setup.** Compare two training schedules for Qwen2.5-VL-7B on Zebra-CoT and 3.3B Janus-Pro robot policies on their respective LIBERO or RLBench demonstrations. Both use a shared encoder trained to support later teacher steps and the final answer or action. Change **when the encoder and generator learn**:

- **Staged training:** first learn the encoder with continuation and final-output supervision. Freeze it, then train the diffusion reasoner to generate its thought blocks.
- **Joint training:** update the shared backbone with continuation, final-output, and diffusion losses during training, so the thought representation and its generator learn together.

Both use the same unified architecture and teacher supervision. Evaluate answer accuracy on **V*, MMVP, MathVista, and EMMA**, and task success on **LIBERO and RLBench**, averaging three seeds and giving each of the six benchmarks equal weight. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 4; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/joint-training.html %}

**Results.** Joint training improves the six-benchmark mean by **12.7% relative to staged training**. The relative gains are **15.0%** on RLBench and **8.8%** on EMMA. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Conclusion.** **Training the encoder and diffusion reasoner together works better than learning and freezing the encoder first.**

This leaves a simpler alternative to diffusion: predict the next clean thought block in one pass.

### RQ5. Does diffusion improve on direct thought prediction? {#rq5}

**Setup.** Compare three thought generators in Qwen2.5-VL-7B trained on Zebra-CoT and 3.3B Janus-Pro robot policies trained on their respective LIBERO or RLBench demonstrations. All use a unified encoder and joint training, with the same supervision from later teacher steps and final outputs, and the same loss weights. Change **the thought-prediction objective and its generation procedure**:

- **L2 prediction:** directly predict a clean block and minimize squared distance to the target.
- **Cosine prediction:** directly predict a clean block and train its direction to align with the target.
- **Flow matching:** learn the velocity along a noisy-to-clean path, then generate a block by integrating from Gaussian noise.

All three receive the same task input and preceding thought blocks. Their predicted blocks feed the downstream answer or action prediction. Measure accuracy on **V*, MMVP, MathVista, and EMMA**, and success on **LIBERO and RLBench**, averaging three seeds and giving each of the six benchmarks equal weight. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 6; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/diffusion.html %}

**Results.** Flow matching improves the six-benchmark mean by **16.3% relative to L2 prediction** and **21.0% relative to cosine prediction**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Conclusion.** **Flow matching produces thoughts that lead to higher average task performance than either direct-prediction objective.**

Finally, we test the generated chain directly by changing its contents while keeping the task input fixed.

### RQ6. Does the final prediction actually use the generated thoughts? {#rq6}

**Setup.** Use Uni-LaDiR with Qwen2.5-VL-7B trained on Zebra-CoT for V* visual questions, and a 3.3B Janus-Pro policy trained on RLBench demonstrations for robot tasks. Within each setting, keep the **trained checkpoint fixed** and generate its thought chain normally. Then compare four conditions, without retraining: keep the chain intact; zero its contents; shuffle whole blocks while preserving token order within each block; or replace it with another example's chain of the same shape.

Keep the task input and sequence length fixed. Recompute the final prediction with fresh hidden states, so it uses the supplied chain. Pair decoding seeds for visual questions. For RLBench, apply the intervention at every reasoning update and pair runs by initial scene and rollout seed. Evaluate **V* answer accuracy** and **RLBench task success**. The comparison tests the contribution of the chain's contents, order, and connection to the current example. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Table 7; Appendix B.4.)

| Generated chain | V* accuracy (%) | RLBench success (%) |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Intact | **89.5** | **87.0** |
| Zeroed | 55.8 | 55.0 |
| Blocks shuffled | 65.8 | 65.0 |
| Another example's chain | 50.8 | 51.0 |

**Results.** Removing the contents, changing their order, or supplying the wrong example's thoughts substantially reduces performance. Replacement lowers V* accuracy from **89.5% to 50.8%** and RLBench success from **87.0% to 51.0%**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Conclusion.** **The model uses the generated thoughts. Their contents, order, and relevance to the current example all matter to the final answer or action.**

## Discussion: the Platonic connection {#discussion}

Why might images, words, and robot states support a common representation at all? They describe overlapping parts of the same world. A relation such as *the hand is above the mug* can appear in a photograph, a sentence, or a 3D scene.

The **Platonic Representation Hypothesis**, proposed by Huh and colleagues, argues that models trained on different data and objectives may converge toward a shared statistical picture of that world. They find that more capable vision and language models tend to agree more about which examples are similar. Their notion of alignment concerns this pattern of similarities across examples; the models can still use different vector coordinates. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>

**Uni-LaDiR turns the idea of a shared space into a training choice for reasoning.** The Platonic hypothesis asks why separately trained models might arrive at similar representations. We ask how to learn a shared representation that helps one reasoning step lead to another. We explicitly share the encoder and judge its thoughts by the continuations and task outputs they support. The experiments test the benefit of that design through answer accuracy and robot success. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

This connection leads to a useful insight: **what representations share depends on what they are asked to do.** The Platonic paper argues that solving more tasks puts stronger constraints on which representations work. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup> In our setting, continuation supplies a concrete constraint. A thought about a mug must preserve information needed for the next stage of the task. Grasping may require clearance and orientation; sorting mugs by color requires color. Broader reasoning tasks could therefore teach a shared space to carry a broader set of useful distinctions.

A shared space also needs room for complementary evidence. An image may show color that a geometric point cloud omits; robot state adds the hand's position. Uni-LaDiR gives these facts a common interface while allowing different thoughts to carry different contents. Each block contributes information from one teacher step; later reasoning combines it with the task input and earlier blocks. **A common space can support many partial thoughts that work together.** This makes unification useful even when the modalities contribute different pieces of the answer.

## What this means for future models {#conclusion}

**Uni-LaDiR makes the targets of intermediate reasoning learnable.** The encoder learns what information the thought blocks should carry, and the diffusion reasoner learns to produce those blocks. Training them together improves visual reasoning and robot control through a shared latent interface. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> This opens up a larger design space for multimodal models: training can shape both the reasoning process and the form in which it happens.

For future training, a valuable unit of supervision could be a *useful transition*: what information lets the model get from its current situation to a better prediction or decision? A written explanation, a video, and a robot demonstration could teach different parts of that transition. With a common thought interface, learning from one source could help a model use another. A concrete test would be whether learning spatial constraints from geometry improves decisions made from images alone. That is the kind of transfer that would make adding a modality improve the model's broader reasoning ability.

For latent reasoning, this creates two things to improve together: **what a thought contains and how the model moves between thoughts**. Future training could reward chains that preserve the right evidence, explore useful alternatives, and spend more computation on decisions that need it. The standard for a good internal representation would then be the reasoning it enables across many tasks and kinds of experience.

The long-term opportunity is to make knowledge learned through different modalities accumulate in the same reasoning system. A fact learned from a video could inform a plan; a constraint learned through action could improve an explanation. **Unification matters most when learning through one modality helps the model think better through the others.** A shared space learned through reasoning gives us a concrete path toward that goal.
