tldr: |
  - **Problem:** An image can show where an object is; words can explain what to do; a robot state can tell us where the hand is. One decision may need all three. Yet intermediate thoughts are often trained to copy each teacher's own format, leaving the model to reason across different representation spaces.
  - **Our solution:** **Uni-LaDiR learns a common space for thoughts.** A shared encoder maps teacher steps into compact latent blocks. **Continuation prediction** teaches each block to retain what helps the next step, rather than reproduce the previous one. **Diffusion** learns to generate these blocks from the input and earlier thoughts. We train the encoder and diffusion reasoner **jointly**, so the learned thought space and its generator can adapt together. At test time, the model generates thoughts without its teachers.
  - **Key results:** Sharing text and image improves four-benchmark mean accuracy by **11.3% relative with the same teachers**. Continuation prediction beats reconstruction by **9.2 percentage points** on the six-benchmark average with a unified encoder. The full system reaches **87% RLBench success at 16.5 Hz** and improves visual math and logic accuracy by **7.3% relative over ILVR**. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19878).

<div id="motivation"></div>

Human thinking connects information across senses. Seeing a dog and hearing it bark can both bring to mind what we know about dogs. The **hub-and-spoke** account in cognitive science offers one explanation: different senses supply different details, while shared conceptual knowledge helps us connect them. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-16">[16]</a></sup><sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-17">[17]</a></sup> **Could an AI model learn from different modalities and carry its reasoning forward in a shared space?**

Consider a robot picking up a mug. An image reveals the handle, a point cloud reveals the clearance around it, and the robot state tells us where the hand is. Planning the grasp requires connecting these facts. Yet a reasoning chain can contain all three and still represent each step in a different space. LaST₀, for example, combines visual, 3D, and state reasoning while retaining separate latent formats. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup> Advancing the chain then means predicting across those modality-specific spaces.

**Why make the reasoning model cross that boundary at every step?** A shared representation would let it carry a useful relation—such as the clearance beside the handle—into the next thought through the same interface, regardless of which modality supplied the evidence. This is the motivation for Uni-LaDiR: learn one space for intermediate thoughts, so successive reasoning steps can use it throughout the chain. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/world.html %}

The question is then how to learn that space. Giving every step the same token shape is straightforward. Deciding what those tokens should remember is the real design choice.

## Learn the shared space through reasoning {#unification}

A natural way to train a representation is to ask it to reproduce its source. An image becomes a latent block that reconstructs visual features; a sentence becomes a block that predicts text. This gives each block a clear target, but the target still comes from its original modality. Mull-Tokens uses modality-agnostic latent tokens with supervision from text tokens or frozen image features. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-15">[15]</a></sup> Our question is whether reasoning itself can teach the shared representation what to keep.

For the mug, reconstructing its appearance and planning a grasp ask for different information. The grasp needs the handle's location and the space available for the hand. **We train each thought to help predict what comes next:** later teacher steps and the final answer or action. This is **continuation prediction**. It shapes the representation around how the information will be used, rather than around how it was first observed. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/decision.html %}

This gives unification a concrete purpose. Different modalities still contribute different evidence, but their thought blocks share an interface and a learning objective. The broader question of whether models converge toward shared representations also motivates the Platonic Representation Hypothesis. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup> Here, we build the space explicitly and train it to carry reasoning forward.

There is one more requirement: at test time, the model must produce these thoughts itself. Uni-LaDiR pairs the shared encoder with a **diffusion reasoner** that generates thought blocks from the task input and earlier thoughts. We train them **jointly**, so the representation learns to support the next step while its generator learns to produce it. To our knowledge, Uni-LaDiR is the first framework to combine a continuation-trained shared thought space with latent diffusion across visual reasoning and robot control. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

[Paper →](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19878)

## The method: thoughts we can use and generate {#method}

A shared thought space has to meet three requirements. Its blocks must use a common format, carry information the next step needs, and be possible to generate from the available context. The three components follow Figure 2: **Latent Encoding**, **Continuation Prediction**, and **Diffusion Training**. We explain each in turn, then show how **Joint Training** brings them together. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/grounding.html %}

### 1. Latent Encoding {#latent-encoding}

Let a **teacher step** be one piece of a training reasoning trace: a sentence, an intermediate image, a point cloud, or a robot state. A modality encoder first turns it into features, denoted <em>s<sub>i</sub></em>. We append learnable queries <em>q</em>, pass the step through the shared LLM encoder, and project the query outputs into a block of thought tokens <em>z<sub>i</sub><sup>★</sup></em>.

Every modality uses the same queries and output projection. In our experiments, a block contains **four 512-dimensional tokens**. An attention mask allows each query block to read only its own teacher step and itself. It cannot read the task input or other teacher steps while constructing that block. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 3.2; Appendix B.2.)

Each block therefore summarizes one local teaching step. The input and earlier blocks provide the wider context when the reasoner uses it. This distinction matters: we are not asking each block to reconstruct a complete world state.

### 2. Continuation Prediction {#continuation-prediction}

A shared shape is easy to specify. Useful contents have to be learned.

We train through **continuation prediction**. Given the task input and the earlier thought blocks, the backbone predicts the next teacher step. Given the entire latent chain, it predicts the final answer or action. Raw earlier teacher steps are hidden from these predictions, as are future latent blocks. Information from a previous teacher step must reach the next prediction through its thought tokens. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 3.3; Appendix A.1.)

For the mug, an earlier block might need to retain a spatial relation that helps predict the next stage of a grasp. Copying the source appearance would reward retaining what makes the image reproducible. Predicting the continuation rewards retaining what makes the later step predictable. The task supplies a reason to keep one detail over another.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/handoff.html %}

The targets remain concrete: text uses cross-entropy; continuous image, 3D, and state targets use squared error. The final output is a text answer for visual reasoning, or an action sequence produced by a flow-matching head for control. The **latent targets are learned**; the teacher targets used for supervision remain fixed.

### 3. Diffusion Training {#diffusion-training}

A teacher can reveal information the model will not have at inference. For example, a future image from a successful demonstration can show that a grasp worked. Encoding it may yield a useful training target, but predicting that target from the current observation is a separate problem.

We use diffusion to learn a conditional distribution over the next thought block:

<div class="uni-equation" role="math" aria-label="Sample the next thought block given the task input and earlier blocks">z<sub>i</sub> ∼ p<sub>θ</sub>(z<sub>i</sub> | x, z<sub>&lt;i</sub>)</div>

Here, <em>x</em> is the task input and <em>z<sub>&lt;i</sub></em> contains the preceding thoughts. The same context can admit several useful continuations. In the mug example, different feasible grasp approaches could require different next steps. Predicting an average with squared-error regression can miss those alternatives. This motivates a distribution over thoughts; diffusion provides a way to generate it while updating all tokens in a block together.

During training, we mix a clean target block with Gaussian noise and train the model to predict the velocity along the noise-to-thought path. This is **flow matching**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-3" aria-label="Reference 3">[3]</a></sup> During inference, we start from noise and integrate the learned updates to produce a block. The input and preceding blocks guide every update. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 3.4.)

At inference, the teacher encoding path is removed. The model generates thought blocks from the input, decides when to stop the chain, and predicts the answer or action. It never needs to render an intermediate image or retrieve a future teacher state.

### Joint Training {#joint-training}

**We train the encoder and diffusion reasoner jointly through a shared backbone.** This matters because learning the encoder first and freezing it would leave the reasoner fitting whatever targets that encoder chose. Those targets may support continuation prediction yet be difficult to generate from the available input.

The joint objective combines **continuation and final-output supervision** with the **diffusion loss**. The former trains the thoughts to support later reasoning and the task output; the latter trains the backbone to generate them. Updating the shared weights also changes how teacher steps are encoded. This couples **what the model needs to retain** with **what it can learn to generate**.

The clean diffusion targets and conditioning prefix are detached from diffusion gradients. Joint training still updates the shared backbone through both losses. It does not pass the answer loss through sampled denoising trajectories. The two objectives shape the shared system through their respective training paths. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>


## Testing the design: six research questions {#experiments}

The full system can improve for several reasons. We separate the practical outcome from the effects of sharing, the learning objective, joint training, and the thought generator. Each question below names the comparison, reports its result, and states what we can conclude.

Visual reasoning uses Qwen2.5-VL-7B <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> and text/image teacher traces from Zebra-CoT. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup> Robot control uses LIBERO <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup> and RLBench <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>, with image and state teachers plus 3D point clouds on RLBench. VLM and VLA are separate model instances using the same design. All reported results and original paper figures come from our [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19878). <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

### RQ1. Does unified latent reasoning improve visual answers and robot actions? {#rq1}

**Setup.** We test the complete system in two settings:

- **Visual reasoning.** Train Qwen2.5-VL-7B on Zebra-CoT traces containing text and intermediate images. Compare with the base model and prior reasoning methods using the same backbone and evaluation questions. Measure final-answer accuracy on **seven vision-centric benchmarks** and **four visual math/logic benchmarks**.
- **Robot control.** Use a 3.3B mixture-of-transformers policy with two Janus-Pro-1.5B experts and an action head. Train on **100 demonstrations per task for ten RLBench tasks**, and **500 demonstrations per suite for LIBERO's four ten-task suites**. Future images and robot states provide teacher steps during training; RLBench also supplies 3D point clouds. The RLBench comparison follows LaST₀'s backbone, demonstration, training, and evaluation protocol.

At test time, both models generate their own thoughts from the task input. Visual teachers and future robot states are training supervision. Robot performance is task success: **20 rollouts per RLBench task** and **50 per LIBERO task**, for each of three seeds. Control frequency is measured on one RTX 4090 under the same timing protocol, without action chunking. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Tables 1–3; Appendix B.1.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/main-results.html %}

**Results.** Seven-benchmark vision accuracy rises from **64.6% to 78.5%** over the base model; Mull-Tokens reaches **74.9%**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-15">[15]</a></sup> On four visual math/logic benchmarks, Uni-LaDiR reaches **49.0%**, versus ILVR's **45.7%**: a **7.3% relative gain**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup>

RLBench success rises from LaST₀'s **82% to 87%**, with nine of ten tasks improved and the remaining task matched. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup> Uni-LaDiR runs at **16.5 Hz**, versus **15.4 Hz** for LaST₀ and **1.1 Hz** for CoT-VLA. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-11">[11]</a></sup> Timing uses the same RTX 4090 without action chunking and excludes simulator stepping and rendering. LIBERO mean success is **99.2%** across four suites. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Takeaway.** The full method improves aggregate performance in both visual reasoning and control, while preserving practical control speed on RLBench. These complete-system comparisons establish the outcome; the following ablations examine the design choices behind it.

### RQ2. Does sharing a thought space help when the teachers stay the same? {#rq2}

**Setup.** Keep every teacher modality available and change **which modalities share an encoder**:

- **Visual reasoning:** compare separate text and image encoders with one shared encoder.
- **RLBench:** compare three separate encoders with pairwise sharing—image + 3D, image + state, or 3D + state—and finally one encoder shared by all three. In each pairwise variant, the remaining modality keeps its own encoder.

Every variant uses the same backbone, training data, teacher supervision, continuation objective, and thought budget: **four 512-dimensional tokens per block**. Each separate encoder has the same architecture as the shared encoder. We measure mean accuracy on **V*, MMVP, MathVista, and EMMA**, and task success on **RLBench**, averaged over three seeds. This comparison changes the sharing arrangement while retaining the same teaching signals. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 4.3; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/results.html %}

**Results.** Sharing text and image increases mean accuracy on V* <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup>, MMVP <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-13">[13]</a></sup>, MathVista <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup>, and EMMA <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-14">[14]</a></sup> from **63.0% to 70.1%**, an **11.3% relative gain**. On RLBench, sharing two modalities reaches **83.5–84.5%** success. Sharing all three reaches **87.0%**, compared with **82.0%** for separate encoders. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 3.)

**Takeaway.** The common interface adds value with the same teachers. More teaching signals alone cannot explain this improvement. Sharing lets the same teaching signals produce a more effective reasoning interface.

The sharing study varies which modalities use a common encoder. Figure 5 asks a complementary question: how do the encoder choice and the learning objective work together?

### RQ3. Unified or separate—and continuation or reconstruction? {#rq3}

**Setup.** Cross **two encoder choices** with **two learning objectives**:

| Variant | How modalities are encoded | What each thought is trained to predict |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Separate + Reconstruction | One encoder per modality | Its source teacher features |
| Unified + Reconstruction | One encoder shared across modalities | Its source teacher features |
| Separate + Continuation | One encoder per modality | Later teacher steps |
| Unified + Continuation | One encoder shared across modalities | Later teacher steps |

All four learn the encoder jointly with the reasoner, using the same data, training steps, four-token blocks, diffusion loss, and final-answer/action supervision. Separate encoders use the same architecture, query structure, and output shape as the shared encoder. A fifth variant, **Frozen (separate)**, fixes the modality encoders and trains only the reasoner.

Evaluate **V*, MMVP, MathVista, EMMA, LIBERO, and RLBench**. The first four measure answer accuracy; the last two measure task success. The six-benchmark mean gives each benchmark equal weight and averages results over three seeds. This lets us compare sharing at a fixed objective, then compare objectives at a fixed encoder design. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 4.4; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/objective.html %}

**Results.** Read the figure in two directions:

- **Unified vs. separate, holding the objective fixed.** Across the six benchmarks, unification improves the mean by **3.5 percentage points** under reconstruction and **6.1 points** under continuation. On RLBench, it raises success from **73.0% to 77.3%** under reconstruction, and from **82.0% to 87.0%** under continuation prediction. The common encoder helps in this setting under either objective. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>
- **Continuation vs. reconstruction, holding the encoder fixed.** Continuation prediction improves the six-benchmark mean by **6.6 percentage points** with separate encoding and **9.2 points** with unified encoding. Learning a thought through its downstream use helps under either encoder design. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 5.)

**Takeaway.** Figure 5 evaluates both the **thought format** and the **objective that learns its contents**. The strongest six-benchmark average comes from combining unified encoding with continuation prediction. Sharing the interface and training it for what comes next are complementary choices.

A useful target still has to be reachable. The teacher can supply information during training that the model must infer on its own at test time.

### RQ4. Should the thought encoder and diffusion reasoner learn together? {#rq4}

**Setup.** Use the unified encoder and continuation objective in both variants, but change **when the encoder and generator learn**:

- **Staged training:** first learn the encoder with continuation and final-output supervision. Freeze it, then train the diffusion reasoner to generate its thought blocks.
- **Joint training:** update the shared backbone with continuation, final-output, and diffusion losses during training, so the thought representation and its generator learn together.

Both use the same unified architecture and teacher supervision. Evaluate answer accuracy on **V*, MMVP, MathVista, and EMMA**, and task success on **LIBERO and RLBench**, with the same three-seed, equally weighted six-benchmark mean. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 4; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/joint-training.html %}

**Results.** Joint training improves the six-benchmark mean by **12.7% relative**, including **15.0% relative** on RLBench and **8.8%** on EMMA. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Takeaway.** Letting the representation and its generator adapt together gives better task performance than fixing the representation first. Joint training turns the encoder and generator into a system that learns together.

This leaves a simpler alternative to diffusion: predict the next clean thought block in one pass.

### RQ5. Does diffusion improve on direct thought prediction? {#rq5}

**Setup.** Keep the unified encoder, joint training, continuation loss, and loss weights fixed. Change **only the objective used to predict the next thought block**:

- **L2 prediction:** directly predict a clean block and minimize squared distance to the target.
- **Cosine prediction:** directly predict a clean block and train its direction to align with the target.
- **Flow matching:** learn the velocity along a noisy-to-clean path, then generate a block by integrating from Gaussian noise.

All three receive the same task input and preceding thought blocks. Their predicted blocks feed the downstream answer or action prediction. Measure accuracy on **V*, MMVP, MathVista, and EMMA**, and success on **LIBERO and RLBench**, using the same three-seed, equally weighted six-benchmark mean. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Paper Figure 6; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/diffusion.html %}

**Results.** Flow matching improves the six-benchmark mean by **16.3% relative to L2 prediction** and **21.0% relative to cosine prediction**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Takeaway.** Diffusion is the stronger thought generator in these comparisons. With the encoder and continuation supervision held fixed, learning to generate thoughts through flow matching leads to stronger answers and actions.

The preceding experiments show how to learn and generate useful thoughts. We can now test their role directly: what happens when we change the generated chain?

### RQ6. Does the final prediction actually use the generated thoughts? {#rq6}

**Setup.** Take the **same trained Uni-LaDiR checkpoint** and generate its thought chain normally. Then compare four conditions, without retraining: keep the chain intact; zero its contents; shuffle whole blocks while preserving token order within each block; or replace it with another example's chain of the same shape.

Keep the task input and sequence length fixed. Recompute the final prediction with fresh hidden states, so it uses the supplied chain. For RLBench, apply the intervention at every reasoning update and pair runs by initial scene and rollout seed. Evaluate **V* answer accuracy** and **RLBench task success**. The comparison tests the contribution of the chain's contents, order, and connection to the current example. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> (Table 7; Appendix B.4.)

| Generated chain | V* accuracy (%) | RLBench success (%) |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Intact | **89.5** | **87.0** |
| Zeroed | 55.8 | 55.0 |
| Blocks shuffled | 65.8 | 65.0 |
| Another example's chain | 50.8 | 51.0 |

**Results.** Removing the contents, changing their order, or supplying the wrong example's thoughts substantially reduces performance. Replacement lowers V* accuracy from **89.5% to 50.8%** and RLBench success from **87.0% to 51.0%**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Takeaway.** The chain carries example-specific information that the final prediction uses, and its order matters. The generated thoughts actively contribute to the answer or action.

## What unified reasoning opens up {#conclusion}

The deeper opportunity is to change what multimodal training is trying to build. Images, language, geometry, and actions each reveal something about the world. If intermediate thoughts are always trained to reproduce those sources, their contents remain tied to the format that taught them. Continuation offers another organizing principle: **learn a representation around what the next decision needs.** Our experiments show that this principle can improve both visual answers and robot actions, and that the resulting thought chains actively contribute to those outputs. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

For future multimodal models, this suggests a way to make diverse training data contribute to a common reasoning process. A written explanation, a video, or a robot demonstration could each teach the model which information matters for a later step. The ambition would be for a spatial relation learned through one kind of experience to become useful in another: learning about clearance from geometry, then using that knowledge when reasoning from an image. A continuation-trained shared space gives us a concrete direction for pursuing that kind of reuse.

It also suggests a broader role for **latent reasoning**. A latent thought can be trained as an intermediate state that supports another inference, prediction, or action. Its value then comes from the reasoning it enables. Future systems could learn when to extend that internal chain, how much computation a decision needs, and when to turn a thought into an explanation or an action. Jointly learning the representation and its generator makes the internal reasoning process itself part of what training can shape.

The long-term goal is a model whose experiences arrive in many forms, but whose reasoning can build on them through a common space. Every new modality would then offer another way to teach the model about the world—and every useful thought could become something the model can build on next.
