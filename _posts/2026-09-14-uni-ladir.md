tldr: |
  - **Problem:** Reasoning can draw on words, images, geometry, and robot states. But keeping each step in its own modality's representation makes the model switch spaces as it thinks.
  - **Our solution:** **Uni-LaDiR** learns one shared latent space for intermediate reasoning. Different modalities teach the model what to keep; later steps and the final answer or action determine what matters.
  - **Key idea:** A thought does not need to be written out or rendered as an image. Diffusion generates the next block of thought tokens directly in the shared space.
  - **Key results:** **+7.3% relative** accuracy over ILVR on four visual math and logic benchmarks; **87%** RLBench success versus LaST₀'s **82%**, at **16.5 Hz**. With the same teachers, unifying text and image improves four-benchmark accuracy by **11.3% relative**.

**Thinking should not have to depend on any one modality.** A picture can show where a mug sits. Words can describe what we want to do with it. Geometry can tell us whether a gripper will fit around it. Each gives us something useful for the same decision. Why should every step of reasoning have to stay in one of those formats?

That is the idea behind **Uni-LaDiR: unified reasoning in a learned latent space**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> We let different modalities teach the model, while keeping its intermediate thoughts in one common representation. The model can then build on what it has learned without turning each thought back into a sentence, an image, or a robot-state vector.

To our knowledge, Uni-LaDiR is the first framework to combine **a shared reasoning space learned through continuation prediction** with **diffusion generation of the thoughts within it**, across both visual reasoning and robot control. The contribution is in the intermediate reasoning: we learn what a thought should contain from what the model needs to do next.

[Read the updated paper →](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19878v3)

## Different views, one decision

Consider the scene in Figure 1: a red mug beside a blue book. Answering “What is beside the book?” mostly requires recognizing the objects and their positions. “Pick up the red mug” asks for more: where to approach, how to place the fingers, and whether the grasp will hold.

The image, 3D geometry, and robot state describe different parts of that problem. Their usefulness comes from putting those parts together. Seeing the mug is not enough to grasp it; a closed gripper is not enough to know that it holds anything.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/world.html %}

Many multimodal reasoners already put different kinds of steps into the same sequence. LaST₀, for example, reasons with latent representations of visual, 3D, and robot-state information. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-8" aria-label="Reference 8">[8]</a></sup> But those representations still live in separate spaces. Advancing from a visual step to a state step also means changing the format in which the next thought is expressed.

Uni-LaDiR keeps the intermediate format shared. An image-derived step and a state-derived step use the same kind of thought tokens, and the reasoner predicts the next block in that space. Different input encoders still handle the raw observations. Unification happens **between reasoning steps**.

There are nearby ideas worth distinguishing. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis studies how representations from different models and modalities can align. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-2" aria-label="Reference 2">[2]</a></sup> Mull-Tokens already uses modality-agnostic latent tokens, but supervises each step toward text tokens or frozen image features. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-16" aria-label="Reference 16">[16]</a></sup> Our choice is to learn the shared thought representation from its usefulness for later reasoning. We do not pick one modality's features as the format that every other modality must adopt.

## Learn what the next step will need

A shared vector size alone does not give us useful reasoning. The model also needs to learn what information belongs in those vectors.

During training, Uni-LaDiR reads a teacher reasoning step and compresses it into a small block of **latent thought tokens**: continuous vectors that the model can use internally. All teacher modalities use the same learnable queries, output projection, and block size. Each block reads only its own teacher step, so it cannot borrow information from later steps. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

We train these blocks through **continuation prediction**. Given the input and earlier thought blocks, the model predicts later teacher steps; given the full latent chain, it predicts the answer or action. Earlier raw teacher steps are hidden from those predictions. The model has to carry useful information through the thought tokens.

For the mug, this means retaining the position or geometry needed for the next decision. Reproducing every detail of the mug's appearance is a different goal. Continuation prediction rewards a representation that helps the reasoning move forward.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/grounding.html %}

The teacher steps are available only during training. A future image or robot-state trace can teach the model about a successful action, but at test time the model has to generate its own thought tokens from the observations it actually has. We therefore train the encoder and diffusion reasoner together: one learns what is useful to preserve, and the other learns how to produce it from context.

## How diffusion generates the next thought

Once thoughts share a space, we still need a way to generate them. We use diffusion: start a block from noise and repeatedly update it, guided by the task input and previous thought blocks. The tokens within the current block are updated together. After completing that block, the model moves on to the next. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

Why use a generative process here? The same situation may allow more than one useful next thought. In our mug example, approaching the handle and approaching the body could both lead to a valid grasp. A predictor trained to minimize squared error tends toward an average; an average of two good options need not be a good option itself. This example motivates learning possible continuations rather than a single mean prediction. It is an illustration, not a decoded model trace.

<div class="uni-equation" role="math" aria-label="The next thought block is sampled given the task input and earlier thought blocks">z<sub>i</sub> ∼ p<sub>θ</sub>(z<sub>i</sub> | x, z<sub>&lt;i</sub>)</div>

Here, <em>x</em> is the task input and <em>z<sub>&lt;i</sub></em> contains the earlier thought blocks. Diffusion produces one possible next block conditioned on both. We train it with **flow matching**, which learns the updates along a noise-to-thought path. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-3" aria-label="Reference 3">[3]</a></sup>

The encoder and reasoner share backbone weights. Continuation prediction shapes the contents of the thought tokens; diffusion learns to generate those tokens. Both objectives update the shared weights, even though diffusion gradients are stopped through the clean targets. This lets the representation and its predictor adapt together. The training does not backpropagate the answer loss through sampled denoising trajectories.

At inference, the model generates thought blocks until it predicts that the chain should end, then produces the answer or action. Intermediate teacher traces are neither supplied nor decoded. Denoising updates the current block; previously completed blocks remain fixed.

## Does unified reasoning help?

We test the same design in two settings, using separate model instances. Visual reasoning uses Qwen2.5-VL-7B <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-4" aria-label="Reference 4">[4]</a></sup>, trained with text and image traces from Zebra-CoT. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-5" aria-label="Reference 5">[5]</a></sup> Robot control uses image and state teachers on LIBERO <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-6" aria-label="Reference 6">[6]</a></sup>, with an additional 3D teacher on RLBench. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-7" aria-label="Reference 7">[7]</a></sup>

### Better answers and robot actions

{% include blog/uni-ladir/main-results.html %}

On **seven vision-centric benchmarks**, average accuracy rises from **64.6% to 78.5%** over the Qwen2.5-VL base model. The paper reports a **21.4% relative gain**, computed before rounding. On **four visual math and logic benchmarks**, Uni-LaDiR reaches **49.0%**, compared with ILVR's **45.7%**: **7.3% higher in relative terms**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">[9]</a></sup> These compare complete methods; the VLM baselines use the same backbone and evaluation examples, but have their own training data and recipes. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Table 2; Appendix B.1.)

On **RLBench**, success rises from LaST₀'s **82% to 87%**. Uni-LaDiR improves nine of ten tasks and matches the remaining one. It runs at **16.5 Hz**, compared with LaST₀'s **15.4 Hz** and CoT-VLA's **1.1 Hz** <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-12" aria-label="Reference 12">[12]</a></sup>, on the same RTX 4090 without action chunking. On **LIBERO**, mean success across four suites is **99.2%**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Tables 1 and 3; timing protocol in Appendix B.2.)

The gains are broad, but they do not mean we win every benchmark: on MathVista <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-10" aria-label="Reference 10">[10]</a></sup>, Uni-LaDiR scores **73.2%**, just below Vision-R1's **73.5%**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-11" aria-label="Reference 11">[11]</a></sup>

<p class="uni-result-takeaway"><strong>What we learn:</strong> the design helps both visual reasoning and robot control. On RLBench, higher success comes with a slightly higher control frequency than LaST₀.</p>

### Same teachers, different sharing

The next question is whether the gain comes from unification itself. We keep the backbone, training data, and teacher supervision fixed, then change which modalities share the encoder. All variants use the same thought-token count and width. Separate encoders have at least as many encoder parameters as the unified model; this is not a parameter-matched comparison. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 4.3; Appendix B.3.)

{% include blog/uni-ladir/results.html %}

Sharing text and image increases mean accuracy on V* <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">[13]</a></sup>, MMVP <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-14" aria-label="Reference 14">[14]</a></sup>, MathVista, and EMMA <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-15" aria-label="Reference 15">[15]</a></sup> from **63.0% to 70.1%**, an **11.3% relative gain**. On RLBench, sharing all three teacher modalities reaches **87.0%**, compared with **82.0%** for separate encoders and **84.5%** for the best two-modality groupings.

<p class="uni-result-takeaway"><strong>What we learn:</strong> with the same teachers, sharing works better than keeping separate encoders. Leaving one modality outside the shared space also leaves some of the gain behind.</p>

### What makes the shared space useful?

The updated paper tests three choices behind the design. Each comparison keeps the relevant architecture and supervision fixed apart from the choice being tested. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Section 4.4; Figures 4–6.)

| Choice | Comparison | Result across six benchmarks |
|:--|:--|:--|
| What should thoughts retain? | Reconstruct the source → predict later steps, using the unified encoder | **+9.2 percentage points** in mean score |
| Should the encoder and reasoner adapt together? | Separate-stage → joint training | **+12.7% relative** mean score |
| How should thoughts be generated? | Direct L2 prediction → flow matching | **+16.3% relative** mean score |

These averages combine four visual benchmarks with LIBERO and RLBench; they summarize the ablations rather than a single task metric. The results support the three choices together: learn thoughts for what comes next, train their representation and generator jointly, and use diffusion to generate them.

We also check whether the model actually uses its generated thoughts. On RLBench, zeroing the latent chain drops success from **87% to 55%**; shuffling the blocks drops it to **65%**. The input and chain length stay unchanged. The contents and order of the chain matter to the final decision. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></sup> (Appendix B.4, Table 7.)

## Conclusion

Uni-LaDiR gives multimodal reasoning a shared intermediate space. Words, images, geometry, and robot states can teach the model without fixing the format in which it has to think. Continuation prediction learns what the thoughts should carry; diffusion learns to generate them.

Our experiments support that choice across visual reasoning and robot control, including comparisons with the same teachers. They also show that the generated thoughts affect the output. The remaining question is how well this space will handle new kinds of teaching signals, such as touch or model-generated traces. For now, the takeaway is simple: **different modalities can contribute to one reasoning process without each thought having to remain tied to its source modality.**
