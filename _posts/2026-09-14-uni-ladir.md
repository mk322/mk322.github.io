## The big idea in 30 seconds {#big-idea}

Modern AI systems increasingly need to reason across **language, images, 3D geometry, and physical states**. Pixels do not look like words, and a point cloud does not look like a robot joint configuration. Yet they may all need to contribute to the same reasoning process.

**Do different modalities need separate reasoning processes, or can their intermediate thoughts share one task-oriented space?**

**Uni-LaDiR** explores the second possibility. Rather than forcing modalities into the same raw representation, it maps their task-relevant information into a shared **latent thought space**. The model learns to generate intermediate thoughts in this space and uses them to predict the answer or action. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

Different modalities do not need to become the same. **Their task-relevant information can contribute through a shared interface for intermediate thoughts.**

</aside>

The argument follows three questions: **what should be shared, what should a thought retain, and how can the model generate it?** The experiments then test each design choice.

[Read the paper →](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19878)

## 1. AI speaks many “languages” {#motivation}

Modern multimodal systems work with diverse forms of information:

- **Language:** abstract concepts and relationships.
- **Images and video:** appearance, spatial context, and change over time.
- **Audio:** sounds and temporal cues.
- **3D geometry:** explicit physical structure.
- **Robot states and actions:** an agent's configuration and interaction with the world.

Each modality has its own structure and strengths. The challenge is **how to reason across them**.

An illustrative reasoning chain:

<div class="uni-inline-chain" role="img" aria-label="Image, then text description, then 3D geometry, then robot state, then action."><span>Image</span><span class="uni-chain-link"><span aria-hidden="true">→</span> Text description</span><span class="uni-chain-link"><span aria-hidden="true">→</span> 3D geometry</span><span class="uni-chain-link"><span aria-hidden="true">→</span> Robot state</span><span class="uni-chain-link"><span aria-hidden="true">→</span> Action</span></div>

If each modality uses a completely separate reasoning representation, every transition becomes a modality-specific interface. As more modalities are introduced, this becomes increasingly complicated.

An alternative lets each modality preserve its input representation while mapping the information needed for reasoning into a common latent space.

This motivates **unified latent reasoning**. The list above describes the broader multimodal challenge; Uni-LaDiR's experiments study text, images, 3D observations, and robot states. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

## 2. A shared space for reasoning {#shared-space}

A shared latent space does not mean all modalities should contain the same information. It provides a **common computational format** in which different information can participate in reasoning.

- A **3D observation** might contribute geometric structure.
- A **robot state** might contribute the agent's current configuration.
- **Language** might contribute a relationship needed to interpret an image.

These pieces of information remain different, but downstream reasoning can operate on them through the same interface.

We call each representation a **latent thought**: a small continuous representation that carries useful information from one reasoning step to the next.

The aim is to **preserve modality-specific information** while providing a **shared reasoning interface**.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/world.html %}

## 3. What should a thought remember? {#thought-memory}

Once we introduce a latent thought space, an important question follows:

**What information should each thought contain?**

One possibility is to train it to reconstruct the observation that produced it. This encourages the representation to preserve information about its source. But reasoning does not always require reconstructing everything that came before: only some details may matter for the next step.

This motivates a different objective, **continuation prediction**:

- **Reconstruction** asks a thought to recover its source observation.
- **Continuation** asks it to support prediction of what comes next.

Rather than asking the representation to describe the past, we ask it to preserve the information needed by the future. It becomes an **information bottleneck between reasoning steps**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, §3.3]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/handoff.html %}

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

A useful thought preserves **what later reasoning needs**, rather than everything about the observation that produced it.

</aside>

## 4. Generating thoughts {#generating-thoughts}

Continuation prediction defines what a useful thought should contain. During inference, however, the model cannot access the teacher's intermediate reasoning steps. It needs to generate its own thoughts.

There are therefore two connected problems:

1. **Learn useful latent representations.**
2. **Learn to generate them from the available context.**

Uni-LaDiR uses a **diffusion model** for the second part. Given the same context, multiple intermediate thoughts may be valid. Instead of directly regressing to one latent target, diffusion learns a distribution over plausible next thoughts. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, §3.4]</a></sup>

The model generates a thought, adds it to the context, and repeats. The resulting chain supports the final prediction.

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

**Latent space unifies the representation of thoughts; diffusion unifies their generation.** Continuation prediction defines what to retain, and diffusion learns how to produce it.

</aside>

With this intuition in place, we can describe the method more precisely.

## 5. Method {#method}

Uni-LaDiR consists of two closely connected components:

- A **latent encoder** converts intermediate multimodal reasoning steps into latent thoughts.
- A **latent generator** learns to generate those thoughts during inference.

Both operate through a **shared model backbone** and are trained jointly. The method diagram also shows continuation prediction, which teaches the encoded thoughts what information to retain. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, Fig. 2]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/grounding.html %}

### 5.1 Encoding multimodal reasoning steps {#latent-encoding}

Each teacher reasoning step can be text, an image, a 3D point cloud, or a robot proprioceptive state. Its corresponding modality representation is processed first; a small set of **learnable query tokens** then collects information from that step through the shared encoder.

The query outputs are projected into a fixed-size latent block, <em>z<sub>i</sub></em> ∈ ℝ<sup>K × D</sup>, where **K** is the number of latent tokens and **D** is their dimension. In the experiments, each thought contains **four 512-dimensional latent tokens**. All modalities use the same queries and output projection. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, §3.2; Appendix B.2]</a></sup>

Each query block attends only to its own teacher step and itself while encoding. The task input and earlier thoughts provide the wider context when the reasoner uses the block. **Sharing the encoder does not mean exposing each block to every teacher step.**

### 5.2 Learning useful thoughts through continuation prediction {#continuation-prediction}

Suppose a teacher trajectory contains steps <em>x<sub>1</sub>, x<sub>2</sub>, …, x<sub>T</sub></em>. The encoder produces <em>z<sub>i</sub> = E(x<sub>i</sub>)</em>.

Rather than reconstructing <em>x<sub>i</sub></em> from <em>z<sub>i</sub></em>, the model uses the **task input and accumulated latent thoughts** to predict later steps.

Raw earlier teacher steps are hidden from these predictions, as are future latent blocks. Information from an earlier step must therefore survive in its thought tokens. The final-output objective also connects the latent chain to **successful end-to-end reasoning**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, §3.3]</a></sup>

### 5.3 Generating latent thoughts with diffusion {#diffusion-training}

Encoded thoughts provide training targets, but the teacher steps that produce them are unavailable at test time. Uni-LaDiR trains a diffusion generator using **flow matching**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, §3.4]</a></sup><sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>

For a target thought <em>z</em>, noise is added at a sampled time <em>t</em>. Conditioned on the task and earlier thoughts, the model learns the velocity along a noisy-to-clean path.

The reasoner starts from **random noise** and iteratively denoises it into a **latent thought**.

The generated thought is appended to the context for subsequent thoughts. Because modalities share the same latent format, **the same generation procedure applies regardless of a thought's source modality**.

### 5.4 Jointly learning the encoder and generator {#joint-training}

A natural alternative is to learn the representation first, freeze it, and then train a generator. But a representation useful for encoding teacher steps may be difficult to generate from the available input.

Uni-LaDiR trains both jointly, allowing the latent space to become both **useful for reasoning** and **feasible to generate**.

The objective combines:

- **Continuation prediction:** preserve information needed by later steps.
- **Final-output prediction:** connect latent reasoning to task performance.
- **Diffusion training:** generate thoughts without teacher intermediates.

The clean targets and earlier thought blocks use **stop-gradient during diffusion updates**. Continuation and final-output losses train the encoded thoughts; diffusion trains their generator. Both update the shared backbone, coupling representation learning and generation. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

### 5.5 Inference {#inference}

Given the input task, Uni-LaDiR:

1. **Initializes** a latent thought from noise.
2. **Denoises** it conditioned on the current context.
3. **Adds** the thought to the reasoning history.
4. **Repeats** for subsequent thoughts until the chain stops.
5. **Predicts** the final answer or action from the latent chain.

Intermediate multimodal supervision defines a useful space during training. At inference, reasoning proceeds through **generated latent thoughts**, without teacher steps. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

## 6. What do the experiments teach us? {#experiments}

The experiments evaluate two settings:

- **Visual reasoning:** answer questions requiring understanding and reasoning over visual information.
- **Robot manipulation:** reason over visual, geometric, and proprioceptive information to produce actions.

These use **separate model instances of the same design**, rather than one model jointly trained across both domains. We first evaluate the full system, then examine four design lessons. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, §4]</a></sup>

### Main results: Better visual answers and robot actions {#main-results}

**Setup.** For visual reasoning, train Qwen2.5-VL-7B on Zebra-CoT traces containing text and intermediate images, and compare against the base model and reasoning methods on the same evaluation questions. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup><sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup> For manipulation, evaluate on RLBench and LIBERO; the RLBench comparison follows LaST₀'s backbone, demonstration, training, and evaluation protocol. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup><sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup><sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup> At test time, the models generate their own latent thoughts without teacher steps.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/main-results.html %}

**Results.** The complete system improves performance in both domains:

- **Visual reasoning:** mean accuracy over seven benchmarks rises from **64.6%** for the base model and **72.2%** for Mirage to **78.5%** for Uni-LaDiR.
- **Visual math and logic:** mean accuracy over four benchmarks rises from **36.4%** for the base model and **40.7%** for Mirage to **49.0%**.
- **Robot manipulation:** RLBench success improves from **82.0%** for LaST₀ to **87.0%**, with nine of ten tasks improved and the remaining task matched. LIBERO mean success is **99.2%** across four suites. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, Tables 1–3]</a></sup>

Uni-LaDiR runs at **16.5 Hz**, versus **15.4 Hz** for LaST₀ under the same RTX 4090 timing protocol, without action chunking and excluding simulator stepping and rendering. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

The full system improves **visual reasoning accuracy and robot task success** over the compared baselines. The following ablations test which design choices make that shared reasoning space effective.

</aside>

### Lesson 1: A shared latent space can improve multimodal reasoning {#lesson-1}

**Setup.** Compare increasingly shared representations while keeping the backbone, data, teacher supervision, continuation objective, and thought budget fixed:

- **Visual reasoning:** separate text and image latent encoders versus a shared encoder.
- **Robot manipulation:** separate encoders versus pairwise sharing and sharing all three modalities—images, 3D point clouds, and robot states. All variants retain every teacher modality.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/results.html %}

**Results.** Increased sharing improves performance in both domains:

- **Visual reasoning:** average accuracy across four benchmarks rises from **63.0% to 70.1%** with shared text and image encoding.
- **RLBench:** success rises from **82.0%** with separate encoders to **83.5–84.5%** with pairwise sharing and **87.0%** with all three shared. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, Fig. 3]</a></sup>

These modalities have very different raw structures. The result suggests that their task-relevant information benefits from a common reasoning representation; it does not make the modalities interchangeable.

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

Different modalities can preserve different information while **benefiting from a shared reasoning interface**.

</aside>

### Lesson 2: Thoughts are more useful when they predict what comes next {#lesson-2}

**Setup.** Compare **reconstruction** and **continuation prediction**, with comparable latent capacity and downstream tasks. Test each objective with separate and unified encoders:

{% include blog/uni-ladir/objective.html %}

**Results.** Continuation prediction performs better across the evaluated visual and robot tasks. Averaged equally over six benchmarks, it improves performance by **6.6 percentage points with separate encoders** and **9.2 points with unified encoding**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, Fig. 5; §4.4]</a></sup>

Reconstruction may retain accurate details that are less useful for reasoning. Continuation prediction instead pressures the bottleneck to keep what later steps need. This is an interpretation of the objective and results, rather than a direct measurement of each latent token's meaning.

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

A thought is useful when it **supports what comes next**. Continuation prediction learns stronger reasoning representations than reconstruction in the evaluated settings.

</aside>

### Lesson 3: The latent space and its generator should be learned together {#lesson-3}

**Setup.** Compare two schedules:

- **Separate training:** learn the latent encoder first, then freeze it while training the generator.
- **Joint training:** optimize the latent encoder and generator together.

{% include blog/uni-ladir/joint-training.html %}

**Results.** Joint training improves the six-benchmark mean by **12.7% relative** to staged training. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, Fig. 4]</a></sup>

A useful thought must retain what later reasoning needs, and the model must be able to generate it from the available context.

A separately trained encoder may produce useful teacher-derived representations that are difficult for the generator to reproduce. Joint learning allows the shared space to adapt to both requirements.

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

**Learn the reasoning space and its generator together.** A thought must both carry useful information and be feasible to generate.

</aside>

### Lesson 4: Diffusion generates more effective thoughts than direct prediction {#lesson-4}

**Setup.** Compare direct latent prediction with **L2** or **cosine** objectives against **flow matching**. All variants use a unified encoder and joint training, with the same continuation and final-output supervision. Each generator receives the task input and preceding thoughts. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, §4.4; Appendix B.3]</a></sup>

{% include blog/uni-ladir/diffusion.html %}

**Results.** Flow matching improves the six-benchmark mean by **16.3% relative to L2 prediction** and **21.0% relative to cosine prediction**. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, Fig. 6]</a></sup>

This is consistent with the idea that several next thoughts can be valid and diffusion can model that distribution. The ablation establishes a performance advantage in these settings; it does not by itself prove that multiple valid continuations explain the entire gain.

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

**How thoughts are generated matters.** Diffusion-based generation achieves stronger downstream performance than direct prediction with L2 or cosine objectives in these experiments.

</aside>

## 7. Sharing is useful because modalities are different {#discussion}

Why give a robot both an image and a point cloud? If they told it exactly the same thing, the second input would add little.

Imagine a mug on a crowded shelf. The image helps identify the mug and its handle. The point cloud provides depth and clearance. The robot's state locates the hand relative to the scene. **The grasp depends on putting these different pieces of evidence together.**

This is what we want from a shared thought space. Each modality contributes what it knows, in a format the next reasoning step can use. Sharing makes the evidence easier to combine; the evidence itself can remain different.

### Common structure—or complementary knowledge?

The **Platonic Representation Hypothesis** asks whether different models and modalities converge toward a common statistical account of the world. It studies what their representations have in common. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup>

We are interested in a different question:

**What can one modality tell the reasoner that another cannot?**

For the grasp, geometry matters because it adds information about space. Robot state matters because it adds information about the agent. A useful thought space should make room for those contributions, rather than preserving only the information all modalities share.

The two perspectives can coexist: shared concepts help connect observations, while complementary evidence helps solve the task. Our experiments test whether sharing the reasoning interface helps; they do not test the Platonic hypothesis itself. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, §4.3–4.4]</a></sup>

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

**The goal is to use what makes each modality different.** A shared thought space lets those differences serve the same reasoning task.

</aside>

## 8. What a thought should remember depends on what comes next {#broader-view}

Now change the task. Instead of grasping the mug, ask the robot to sort it by color.

The observation is the same, but the useful information changes:

| Next task | What the thought may need to retain |
|---|---|
| Grasp the mug | Handle position, clearance, hand configuration |
| Sort by color | The mug's color and the corresponding destination |

A representation trained to reconstruct the observation has the same target in both cases. **A representation trained to support the next step is rewarded for keeping what later reasoning actually uses.**

That is the broader lesson we take from continuation prediction. A latent thought is useful because it enables the next computation, not simply because it is a compact copy of an earlier step. The reconstruction-versus-continuation ablation supports this distinction in our evaluated tasks. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, Fig. 5]</a></sup> This example illustrates the learning objective; it is not a decoding of the model's actual thoughts.

### Beyond hiding the words

Latent reasoning already offers several ways to compute without writing every step. **Implicit CoT** distills teacher reasoning into hidden states; **Coconut** carries continuous thought states forward. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-13">[13]</a></sup><sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-11">[11]</a></sup> But removing words still leaves a question: **what should the latent computation preserve?**

Mirage provides a related example. It first grounds visual latents in image embeddings, then relaxes that constraint so the latents can adapt to answering the question. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup> Uni-LaDiR makes future reasoning steps part of the supervision, giving the thought space a direct reason to preserve information useful for continuation.

There is one more requirement: the model has to produce those thoughts without the teacher. LaDiR learns a thought space and then a diffusion generator. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup> Our joint-training result suggests that, in the multimodal setting we study, the space benefits from adapting to the generator as well. <sup class="uni-cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1, Fig. 4]</a></sup>

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

**A thought should carry what the next step needs—and be something the model can actually generate.** Those two requirements should shape the latent space together.

</aside>

## 9. Can one modality teach another to reason? {#conclusion}

So far, sharing helps information from different modalities contribute to a reasoning chain. A more ambitious possibility is that **experience in one modality could improve reasoning in another**.

Suppose a model learns about clearance from 3D observations. Later, it sees only a photograph. Could that geometric experience help it judge whether a hand can fit behind the mug?

That would be more than accepting two input formats. Knowledge acquired through one kind of experience would become useful when that modality is absent.

The same question appears elsewhere:

- Could **physical interaction** help a visual model recognize what can be grasped or moved?
- Could **language abstractions** help an agent recognize the same relationship in unfamiliar scenes?

Uni-LaDiR does not yet establish this transfer: its visual and robot models were trained separately. But it suggests a place to look—a thought space trained around the reasoning task, where complementary evidence can be used through a common interface.

For future multimodal models, we would pursue **specialized perception, shared task-directed thoughts, and joint learning across forms of experience**. The decisive experiment would compare joint and separate learning under matched data and compute, then test reasoning when one modality is missing.

<aside class="uni-key-message" aria-label="Key message" markdown="1">

**Key message**

**The next goal is for learning through one modality to improve reasoning through another.** A shared thought space offers a possible route; demonstrating that transfer remains the test.

</aside>
